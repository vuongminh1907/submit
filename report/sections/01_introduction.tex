\section{Introduction}

\paragraph{Context.}
Orange operates large national and international IP/MPLS backbones containing thousands of routers
and links and carrying many thousands of origin--destination traffic demands. The continued growth
of traffic, the diversity of services, and the development of Software-Defined Networking and Network
Virtualization make the efficient use of this infrastructure increasingly important~\citep{orange2026}.
Traffic Engineering (TE) addresses this operational problem by selecting routing parameters and paths
so that the available capacity is used efficiently while an acceptable Quality of Service (QoS) is
maintained. In particular, routing should avoid local congestion: an overloaded link can degrade every
service whose traffic crosses it even when unused capacity remains elsewhere in the network.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Capacity efficiency alone is not sufficient. Operator networks must also remain available when
equipment is taken out of service, whether because of an unforeseen failure or a planned maintenance
intervention. The subject recalls the standard requirement that a network remain functional after any
single link or node failure, while current security and climate-related risks further motivate
resilience to wider disruptions~\citep{orange2026}. Traffic volumes and equipment availability also
vary with time. A routing plan should therefore anticipate forecast traffic matrices and scheduled
interventions rather than optimize only one static nominal state. At the same time, excessive
reconfiguration is undesirable: adding or removing waypoints may require operational actions, can
incur a cost, and can temporarily affect QoS. The problem must consequently balance congestion
reduction, resilience, and routing stability.

Traditional IP routing forwards packets along shortest paths induced by administrative link weights.
This mechanism is simple and scalable, but the set of weights may not provide enough flexibility to
distribute traffic well under several traffic and availability scenarios. Segment Routing
(SR)~\citep{rfc8402} introduces a limited sequence of node segments, or waypoints, into the packet
header. Between two consecutive waypoints, forwarding still follows the existing shortest-path
mechanism. SR can therefore steer a demand through a non-shortest end-to-end route without requiring
a separate forwarding state for every demand at every router, providing additional TE flexibility
with limited implementation overhead.

\paragraph{Problem description.}
The EURO/ROADEF 2026 challenge defines the $T$-Adaptive Segment Routing problem
($T$-ASR)~\citep{orange2026}. Its input is a capacitated directed network with administrative
weights, a set of demands and time-dependent traffic volumes, a planning horizon
$T=\{0,\ldots,h-1\}$, scheduled link interventions, a reconfiguration budget, and a maximum number of
segments. For every demand and every time period, the decision is a segment path from the demand
source to its destination. The selected paths must satisfy flow conservation and the segment limit;
the induced traffic must respect the available period graph; and consecutive routing schemes must
remain within the reconfiguration budget. The resulting link load is the carried flow divided by link
capacity and may exceed one when the network is congested.

The full challenge objective sorts all link loads across all periods in non-increasing order and
minimizes this vector lexicographically: first the largest load, then the second largest, and so
forth~\citep{orange2026}. The implementation studied here solves the scalar primary stage, the
minimization of maximum link utilization (MLU). This is the first component of the lexicographic
objective and a standard TE criterion; its scientific rationale and precise scope are detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:objective}.

\paragraph{Scope of this report.}
Following the course instructions, the model is first evaluated on period~0 alone, which is the
nominal network without the reconfiguration-budget constraint. It is then extended to two periods:
period~1 applies the scheduled link withdrawals, uses its own traffic volumes, and activates the
budget constraint between the two routing schemes. All twenty Set~A instances are tested with a time
limit of 1800\,s for period~0 and 3600\,s for the two-period model. Each reported result is an average
over ten runs with seeds 2026--2035. The remainder of the report presents the data construction,
mathematical formulation, solution method, experimental protocol, numerical results, and limitations
of the scalar MLU study.
